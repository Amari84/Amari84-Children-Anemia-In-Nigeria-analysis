# Children's Anemia in Nigeria: Socioeconomic Analysis

## Project Overview
This project analyzes the relationship between household socioeconomic factors (wealth, maternal education, residence type) and childhood anemia prevalence among Nigerian children aged 0–59 months, using data from the 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS).

## Key Findings
- **68.8%** of Nigerian children under 5 years experience some level of anemia
- **Wealth gap:** Anemia prevalence is 79.5% in poorest households vs. 53.5% in richest (26-point gap)
- **Education gap:** 75.8% for children of uneducated mothers vs. 49.0% for educated mothers (26.8-point gap)
- **Urban-rural gap:** 72.9% in rural areas vs. 62.3% in urban areas
- **Severity matters:** Poorest children don't just have more anemia, their anemia is more likely to be moderate or severe

## Research Question
**How are household socioeconomic factors (wealth, maternal education, residence type) associated with anemia prevalence among children aged 0–59 months in Nigeria?**

## Methodology
- **Sample:** 10,182 children with valid hemoglobin-based anemia readings from a nationally representative survey
- **Approach:** Descriptive group comparisons (percentages within each category)
- **Outcome:** Child anemia level (Not anemic / Mild / Moderate / Severe)
- **Predictors:** Household wealth, maternal education, residence type (urban/rural)

## Key Takeaways
1. **This is an equity problem.** Anemia in Nigeria is concentrated among children from poor, rural, and less-educated households.
2. **Prevention requires targeting.** Prevention programs (iron supplementation, bed nets, fortification) should prioritize the poorest households and rural areas first for maximum impact.
3. **Multiple factors intersect.** The associations suggest that education, income, healthcare access, and infection burden all play roles.
4. **Data shows the way.** Clear socioeconomic gradients provide a roadmap for where public health efforts should focus.

## Limitations
- **Cross-sectional design** : Cannot establish causation, only association
- **Missing data** : 70% of the original dataset lacked valid anemia readings
- **Unmeasured confounders** : Cannot account for all factors affecting anemia (diet quality, infection history, etc.)
- **No design adjustment** : DHS clustered sampling design not accounted for in analyses
